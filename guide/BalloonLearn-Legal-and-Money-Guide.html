Part 1 — The legal documents

1.1 The three names, and what they really mean

People use these terms loosely. Here is the practical difference.

DocumentWhat it doesDo you need it?
Privacy Policy Tells users what personal information you collect, why, who you share it with, and how they can contact you. REQUIRED Google Play will not publish your app without a working link — no exceptions for children's apps.
Terms of Use
(also called Terms & Conditions, T&C, or EULA)		 The rules for using your app: what you allow, what you don't promise, and who owns the software. OPTIONAL Google supplies a default licence agreement if you have none. Strongly recommended anyway — see 1.3.
Terms & Conditions Nothing separate. It is simply another name for Terms of Use. You do not need both. SAME THING Write one document and title it "Terms of Use".
So you need two documents in total: a Privacy Policy (required) and a Terms of Use (recommended). Both are already drafted for you in Part 2.

1.2 Why your Privacy Policy is unusually easy

Most apps struggle here because they collect data. Balloon Learn Kids does not, which makes your policy short, honest and easy to defend:

  • The app does not request the INTERNET permission at all, so it is technically incapable of sending anything anywhere. Anyone can verify this in your manifest.
  • The only permission requested is VIBRATE, for the small buzz when a balloon pops.
  • No advertising library, no analytics library, no crash reporting, no social logins.
  • The only things stored are on the device itself: the sound and vibration switches, speed and size settings, which hints have been seen, and a short list of scores. All of it disappears when the app is uninstalled.

Your project already contains PRIVACY.md saying exactly this. Part 2 gives you a slightly expanded version formatted for public hosting.

1.3 Why bother with Terms of Use if it is optional?

Three practical reasons, none of them about lawsuits:

  • It limits your liability. It states plainly that the app is provided as-is and that you are not responsible if a device misbehaves. Without it, expectations are undefined.
  • It states who owns the app. Useful if someone copies your work and you need to point at something.
  • It looks professional. Parents and reviewers notice. A store listing with both documents linked reads as a serious product.

1.4 A word on "free policy generator" websites

Sites offering instant generated policies are popular, and some are fine. Two cautions:

  • Many generate a policy describing data collection your app does not do. Publishing a policy that overstates collection is worse than none — Google compares it against your app's actual behaviour in the Data Safety review, and a mismatch causes rejection.
  • Several offer a free document but then host it on their domain, and start charging later or take the page down. Your Play listing then links to a dead page, which is a policy violation.

Recommendation: use the templates in Part 2 and host them yourself (Part 3). They are accurate for your app, cost nothing, and no third party can take them offline.

Part 3 — Publishing your documents free

3.1 What the link has to do

Google requires that your privacy policy link:

  • opens directly to the policy — not a home page, a login screen or a file download;
  • is publicly readable with no account required (test it in a private browsing window);
  • keeps working. A dead link after publication can get the app removed.

3.2 Recommended: GitHub Pages FREE, INDEFINITE

Your code is already on GitHub. Pages turns a folder in that repository into a website at no cost, with no expiry and no adverts. This is the most durable free option available.

Steps, once

  1. In your repository create a folder named docs.
  2. Put two files in it: privacy.md and terms.md, containing the text from Part 2.
  3. Go to Settings → Pages. Under "Build and deployment", set Source to Deploy from a branch, branch main, folder /docs. Save.
  4. Wait two or three minutes. Your pages appear at:
    https://meetpatel2306.github.io/BalloonLearnKids/privacy
    https://meetpatel2306.github.io/BalloonLearnKids/terms
  5. Paste the privacy link into the Play Console, and both links into your store listing description.

Simpler alternative, no setup

A file already in your repository is publicly viewable straight away, for example https://github.com/Meetpatel2306/BalloonLearnKids/blob/main/PRIVACY.md. Google accepts this. It is less tidy than a Pages URL but works immediately and costs nothing.

Part 4 — Google Play compliance for a children's app

4.1 The extra rules that apply to you

Because Balloon Learn Kids targets under-fives, it is reviewed against Google's Families policy in addition to the normal rules. In practice that means:

  • Your privacy policy link must be present and working.
  • Your Data Safety answers must match what the app actually does.
  • Any advertising must come from a certified child-safe network and must be non-personalised.
  • Nothing may take a child out of the app without a parental gate — external links, purchases and settings all count.
  • Content must be appropriate for the declared age band.

Balloon Learn Kids already satisfies all of these, including the parental gate: the settings panel is protected behind a simple arithmetic question that an adult can answer and a toddler cannot.

4.2 Exact answers for the Play Console forms

The single most common rejection for small apps is a Data Safety form that does not match the app's real behaviour. Yours is simple because every answer is "no". Keep it that way, and if you ever add an advertising library, change these answers and the privacy policy on the same day.

4.3 Sound and image licences

Everything drawn in Balloon Learn Kids is generated by code, so no image licences apply. The fourteen animal recordings came from Pixabay, whose licence permits commercial use without attribution. Keep the record of each file's source in ANIMAL_SOUNDS.md, and confirm each file is still available under that licence before you publish. If a contributor withdraws a file, replace it — the app falls back to the spoken animal name automatically, so nothing breaks.

The governing equation is simple: income equals installs multiplied by how well you monetise. At a thousand installs, no monetisation method earns meaningfully. At a hundred thousand, all of them do. This is why Part 6 matters more than this part.

5.2 Advertising, in detail

The rules you cannot break

  1. Certified networks only. For a child-directed app the practical choice is Google AdMob configured for families. Other certified options include SuperAwesome and Kidoz.
  2. Non-personalised only. Every request must be tagged as child-directed and rated G. No behavioural targeting, no advertising identifier. This restriction is precisely why the income per view is low.
  3. Placement. No advert a child can tap by accident during play, nothing that resembles a game button, nothing that interrupts at a random moment.
  4. Consequences for the app. Adding an advertising library means adding the internet permission, rewriting your privacy policy, and changing your Data Safety answers to declare what the library collects.

What the money actually looks like

Child-directed banner adverts typically earn roughly $0.50 to $2.00 per thousand views.
A thousand children playing daily produces roughly three to five thousand banner views a day.
That is approximately $2 to $10 per day, or $60 to $300 per month — at a thousand daily players.

Most small children's apps never reach a thousand daily players without deliberate marketing.

The trade-off worth thinking about

The strongest line in your store listing today is that the app is completely offline, collects nothing and shows no adverts — and parents of toddlers search for exactly that. Adding advertising removes your main differentiator and adds review risk, in exchange for modest income. Consider it only once the app is large.

If you decide to do it

  1. Create a free AdMob account and register the app; you receive an App ID.
  2. Create a single banner ad unit.
  3. In the app: add the ads dependency, put the App ID in the manifest, set the child-directed and G-rating flags globally, and place one banner only on adult-facing screens — never over the play area.
  4. Link AdMob to your Play Console and add payment details. Payment arrives monthly once you pass the threshold, typically about $100.
  5. Update your privacy policy and Data Safety form the same day the advert goes live.

5.3 The approach most successful children's apps use

Keep the app free of advertising, and earn from parents who choose to pay.

A one-time unlock, not a subscription

  • Free for ever: Colours, the first ten letters, numbers one to ten.
  • One payment of about $2–3 unlocks: the full alphabet, numbers to twenty, Shapes, Animals, and any future packs.
  • Placement: behind the same arithmetic gate that guards settings — this is what the Families policy requires.
  • Realistic conversion: one to three per cent of engaged parents buy. Ten thousand installs at two per cent and $2.99 is roughly $600, and it grows with installs rather than with usage.

Supporting routes that cost nothing to set up

  • Donations: add a GitHub Sponsors button and a "buy me a coffee" link to your repository and store listing.
  • A supporter edition: the identical app published a second time at a small price, described honestly as a way to support development. No code changes at all.
  • Other stores: the same file published to the Amazon Appstore reaches Fire tablets, which are extremely common among young families. Samsung Galaxy Store and Huawei AppGallery cost nothing to join.
  • Licensing: nurseries and daycare chains occasionally pay a few hundred dollars for a branded version. Your vector artwork makes reskinning quick.

Part 6 — Marketing on a zero budget

This is where income is actually decided. None of the following costs money.

6.1 Store listing optimisation — do this first

  • Title. "Balloon Learn Kids" is memorable but carries only one search word. Use the full thirty characters: Balloon Learn Kids: ABC 123 Kids.
  • Short description. Every word is searchable. Lead with what parents type: toddler, ABC, 123, offline, free, no ads.
  • First three lines of the long description decide most installs. Put the benefits there, not the feature list.
  • Screenshots persuade more than text. Add a short caption over each one: "Says every letter out loud", "Five learning games", "No adverts, works offline".
  • Translate the listing — not the app, just the store text — into Hindi, Spanish, Portuguese and German. Each language opens a new search market at zero cost.
  • Reply to every review. Visible replies improve both ranking and trust.

6.2 Short video, posted everywhere

Record fifteen to thirty seconds of the best moments: the rainbow arch popping, a letter flying into its place, the colour flooding the screen, the fireworks. Post the same clip to YouTube Shorts (marked "made for kids"), Instagram Reels, TikTok, Facebook Reels and Pinterest.

Write captions as a parent, not as a developer: a relatable problem followed by the result. Post three or four times a week. This costs only minutes because the footage is the game itself, and a single clip that travels can produce thousands of installs.

6.3 Communities — helpful, never spammy

  • Parenting forums and subreddits about toddlers and preschoolers. Read each group's self-promotion rules first, and post as a parent-developer sharing something they made, not as an advertiser.
  • Developer communities respond well to the build story: an offline, no-data, open-source children's app is genuinely interesting to them.
  • Your own family and friends groups. The first fifty reviews move the ranking more than any later fifty.

6.4 Free press and directories

Send a short note to sites that review children's apps, describing what the app is and why it is different — offline, no data collected, no adverts, open source — with two screenshots attached. A single listing on a respected review site brings steady installs for years.

6.5 Local and offline

  • Ask nearby nurseries, playgroups and paediatric clinics to share the link in their parent groups. A single printed page with a QR code works well and costs pennies.
  • Libraries with parent-and-toddler sessions will often let you demonstrate it.

6.6 Keeping momentum

  • Update monthly, even slightly. Fresh apps rank better, and every update is new material to post.
  • Use the seasons. Snow in December, pumpkins in October, festival colours — each is a reason for a new video.
  • Ask to be featured. Google's editorial team does feature polished family apps, and you can nominate your own app in the Play Console. A feature is worth tens of thousands of installs, and your no-adverts, no-data design is exactly the kind of thing they look for.
A realistic weekly routine, one to two hours: post one clip on Monday; answer reviews and join one community conversation on Wednesday; post a second clip and check your statistics on Friday. Ship one small update a month.

7.2 Launch checklist

Legal

  • Privacy Policy written, name and email filled in
  • Terms of Use written, country and owner filled in
  • Both published and opening correctly in a private browsing window
  • Sound licences recorded in ANIMAL_SOUNDS.md

Store

  • Developer account created and identity verified ($25)
  • Signing key created and backed up in two places
  • Data Safety form completed — every answer "no"
  • Target age set to 5 and under; content rating completed
  • Icon (512×512), feature graphic (1024×500), at least two screenshots
  • Title, short description and long description written

After launch

  • Ask friends and family to install and review
  • Begin the weekly posting routine
  • Reply to every review
  • Ship a small update in the first month
The one irreversible mistake: losing your signing key. Without it you can never update the app again, and there is no appeal. Copy the key file and its passwords to at least two separate places — a USB stick and a password manager — before you upload anything.
